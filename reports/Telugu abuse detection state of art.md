# Your Telugu detector is solid, not state of the art

**Verdict: the detector is a well-executed, current-standard system, not a modern or advanced one.** Its modeling core (XLM-R-base with LoRA, plus a TF-IDF baseline) matches what Telugu and Indic abuse papers already did in 2022-2025 and is behind the 2025-2026 frontier. Its engineering and operations layer (calibration, allow/review/block routing, canonicalization, INT8 CPU serving) is the strongest part and fits the 2026 literature on deferral and robustness. The notes cannot place the 0.904 macro-F1 against published MACD Telugu numbers, because no per-language MACD table and no 2024-2026 paper reporting MACD Telugu were retrieved. They also found no source for TF-IDF parity, LoRA-versus-full-fine-tune parity, or obfuscation guards in Indic abuse work, so those findings are your own empirical results, not replications of literature. The literature research behind this verdict is thin: roughly 8 to 10 searches per note, mostly snippets, abstracts only, and almost no results tables. The honest summary is "mainstream and defensible; the claim to modernity rests on the deployment layer, and it needs a few comparisons before a reviewer would accept it."

## The modeling core matches 2022-2025 practice

LoRA on encoders is routine. A Feb 2025 paper on code-mixed Telugu-English hate speech already fine-tuned seven transformers with LoRA ([arXiv 2502.10632](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10632)). A survey of low-resource hate speech detection lists XLM-R among the widely used models for Indian languages ([arXiv 2411.19017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.19017)). Evidence from other languages points to near-parity between LoRA and full fine-tuning, with large compute savings: a Persian comparison found LoRA and DoRA very competitive with full fine-tuning ([JAD](https://jad.shahroodut.ac.ir/article_3392.html)), and a multilingual news study found little quality loss from PEFT ([arXiv 2308.07282](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.07282)). Your "LoRA equals full fine-tune" result is therefore plausible and consistent with that evidence. It is not a finding. At about 278M parameters, XLM-R-base is cheap to fully fine-tune on one GPU, so LoRA is a convenience choice. Claim it for efficiency, not accuracy, and keep the full fine-tune as a reported control.

The backbone is the weaker choice. XLM-R-base is a 2019-era encoder, and the Telugu evidence favors Indic-specialized models. The IndicBERT v2 paper reportedly puts the IndicXTREME Telugu average at about **70.2 for IndicBERT, 64.2 for MuRIL and 56.4 for XLM-R**, but that figure came from a search summary and was not checked against the PDF ([arXiv 2212.05409](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.05409)). In the Telugu hate paper, the abuse-tuned Hindi-Abusive-MuRIL was best, and the authors of MACD released AbuseXLMR, which they claim beats XLM-R and MuRIL on four Indic datasets ([NeurIPS 2022](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/a7c4163b33286261b24c72fd3d1707c9-Abstract.html)). Two caveats apply. The Telugu hate paper's own scores look odd (plain MuRIL near 0.42, IndicBERT near 0.37 on translated data), so treat it as one weak data point. And on a perplexity model card XLM-R scored better than MuRIL on Telugu, which is not a classification metric ([HF](https://huggingface.co/SpireLab/RESPIN_LanguageModels/blob/main/README.md)). Notably, your git log shows you already shipped a quantized MuRIL INT8 model, so the MuRIL comparison may be partly done and should be written up.

The "modern encoder" gap is real but unproven. mmBERT (ICML 2026) is a ModernBERT-style encoder trained on 3T+ tokens in 1,800+ languages, and its authors claim large gains over prior multilingual encoders; that claim is author-reported ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.06888)). AI4Bharat lists IndicBERT-v3 encoders (270M, 1B, 4B) with no benchmarks or dates found ([HF](https://huggingface.co/ai4bharat)). No source reports toxicity or Telugu scores for either. Not using them is a defensible gap, but they are the obvious "2026" comparators and a cheap swap.

## TF-IDF parity is your finding, and a double-edged one

The notes found **no source showing TF-IDF+SVM matching transformers on Telugu**. The related evidence is mixed. A 38-model English evaluation found SVM and CatBoost remain competitive, with F1 above 88% on some data ([arXiv 2509.14266](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.14266)). In Tamil caste hate speech at LT-EDI 2025, the domain-tuned Tamilhate-BERT won with macro-F1 0.88 over TF-IDF models, whose scores were not retrieved ([ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2025.ltedi-1.18.pdf)). Parity on a short, lexically driven binary task is believable, and the MACD Telugu subset (about 30K comments, 52% abusive) is exactly that ([NeurIPS 2022](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/a7c4163b33286261b24c72fd3d1707c9-Abstract.html)).

Read parity as a diagnosis of the benchmark, not a compliment to the model. When a linear model on character n-grams ties a transformer, either the labels are mostly decided by surface lexical cues, the labels are noisy and cap everyone at a ceiling, or the split leaks near-duplicates across train and test. Any of these makes 0.904 a weak signal of modeling quality. This is the most important point in the report: a stress test the classical model cannot pass is the only way to show the transformer earns its cost.

## What is genuinely good: the deployment and risk layer

INT8 dynamic quantization is mainstream: ONNX Runtime documents dynamic quantization as the recommended path for transformers and supports it on CPU without retraining ([ORT docs](https://onnxruntime.ai/docs/performance/model-optimizations/quantization.html)). That makes the INT8 ONNX export "current standard, well-executed", not advanced. Attention layers can be quantization-sensitive, so the export should be validated against the FP32 model on a frozen test set; one anecdotal source says this, and your own comparison is the proof.

The calibration and routing design is the part that reads as current. A double-threshold policy of auto-accept, human review and auto-reject is the established pattern ([arXiv 2601.05974](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05974v1)). Learning-to-defer work warns that static thresholds ignore model uncertainty and reviewer capacity ([arXiv 2402.12237](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.12237v3)). May 2026 work argues deferral only works when confidence tracks correctness uniformly across groups ([arXiv 2605.14074](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.14074)), and a June 2026 paper finds that the same content gets different moderation actions in clean English versus code-mixed form ([arXiv 2606.05654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.05654)). Temperature scaling feeding a review band is therefore aligned with where the field is moving. The notes flag that the attribution of the calibration finding to 2601.05974v2 came from a search summary, and the vendor rule of thumb of about 0.90 for auto-accept is unvalidated, so your thresholds should be justified from your own holdout data.

Anti-obfuscation canonicalization also fits the literature, but with thin support. Prior work shows phonetic and transliteration-swap attacks on code-mixed text ([arXiv 2111.00350](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.00350), sentiment not hate), and a DravidianLangTech 2026 Telugu-English paper reportedly notes obfuscation via spelling variants and Romanization ([ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2026.dravidianlangtech-1/)). No Telugu-specific standard exists, and zero-width and homoglyph handling is unsourced engineering practice. It is good practice, but not a validated technique until you measure it. FastAPI plus Streamlit is ordinary, and nothing in the notes bears on it.

## Where the field has moved, and where the notes cannot say

The newest Telugu work remains small-data and encoder-centric. DravidianLangTech 2026 had a hybrid-transformer Telugu-English paper that combines TF-IDF and FastText features with transliteration normalization ([ACL](https://aclanthology.org/2026.dravidianlangtech-1.1/)), and X-MuTeST (AAAI 2026) adds LLM reasoning and explainability-guided training with token-level rationales including 4,492 Telugu samples, though only a snippet was read ([arXiv 2601.03194](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03194)). The Devanagari shared task found few-shot Gemma-2 9B about equal to fine-tuned IndicBERT v2 (F1 0.7588 versus 0.7582), with an ensemble best at 0.7652 ([arXiv 2411.06850](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.06850v1), Nov 2024). Instruction-tuned Indic LLMs lagged on code-mixed Hindi-English ([ACL](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2025.chipsal-1.5)). Together these suggest encoders remain competitive and that LLMs are a reference and ensemble member, not a replacement, though none of it is Telugu-specific. Guard models such as Qwen3Guard ([arXiv 2510.14276](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.14276v1)) and PolyGuard ([arXiv 2504.04377](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.04377v1)) target prompt and response safety, not comment-level abuse, and no Telugu results were found, so they are adjacent rather than competitors.

The table below summarizes the verdict by component.

| Component | Assessment | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|
| XLM-R-base backbone | Generic, dated; Indic models likely stronger | Moderate; Telugu abuse head-to-head missing |
| LoRA r=8 | Routine; parity expected | Moderate, indirect |

Several things are unverified and should not be repeated as fact: the IndicXTREME numbers, the Telugu hate paper's scores, Sarvam-30B/105B claims (secondary sources only; Sarvam models are generative and too heavy for CPU anyway), and anything about DoRA, PiSSA, distillation, SetFit or GGUF, which were not searched.

## Steps that would earn the word "advanced"

First, fix the evidence. Read the MACD NeurIPS results table directly and place 0.904 against it, check that your label mapping matches the reported 0 = abusive, 1 = non-abusive convention, and deduplicate and check for train-test leakage, since TF-IDF parity makes leakage a live concern. Report macro-F1 with confidence intervals over several seeds, and add per-class precision and recall.

Second, run the missing comparators on the same split: MuRIL (likely already trained), IndicBERT v2, AbuseXLMR, and mmBERT, plus a character n-gram TF-IDF baseline tuned fairly, a full fine-tune control, and a few-shot Gemma or Qwen reference. If a stronger Indic encoder beats XLM-R, switch backbones; the serving stack does not change, since LoRA is merged before export.

Third, make the robustness claims measurable. Build a small Telugu stress set with obfuscation (spacing, zero-width characters, homoglyphs, mixed script), Romanized Telugu, and code-mixed Telugu-English variants, and show metrics with and without canonicalization, and for TF-IDF versus the transformer. Consider Roman-to-Telugu transliteration with IndicXlit ([AIKosh](https://aikosh.indiaai.gov.in/home/models/details/indicxlit.html)), as the Hinglish MoH pipeline did ([MoH](https://deepai.org/publication/ceasing-hate-withmoh-hate-speech-detection-in-hindi-english-code-switched-language)).

Fourth, report the operations layer the way 2026 papers do: expected calibration error before and after temperature scaling, a coverage-versus-accuracy curve for the review band, review-queue volume at the chosen thresholds, thresholds fixed on a frozen holdout, and INT8-versus-FP32 agreement and CPU latency. Check calibration across subgroups such as Romanized versus native script, in line with the uniform-confidence concern in the 2026 deferral work. Beyond that, consider targeted LLM augmentation of the minority class (about +4-6% F1 in low-resource settings and +1-2% on full data, abstract-level only ([Springer 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00607-025-01518-8))), which is likely small on 30K examples, and a distilled or ensembled model only if the comparisons show headroom.

## Conclusion

The practical insight is that the project's value sits where its accuracy claims are weakest. Because a linear TF-IDF model ties the transformer, headline macro-F1 cannot differentiate this system from a 2022 baseline, whereas calibrated deferral, obfuscation handling and cheap CPU serving are what make it deployable, and they are the parts the 2026 literature is just starting to emphasize. The strongest honest framing is "a calibrated, robust, CPU-deployable Telugu moderation pipeline", not "a state-of-the-art Telugu model".

What would change this verdict is evidence the notes could not supply: a published MACD Telugu table showing 0.904 is at or above prior results, an mmBERT or IndicBERT-v3 run that beats XLM-R, or a stress test where canonicalization plus the transformer clearly outperforms TF-IDF. Until those exist, the comparison with recent papers rests on thin snippets and inference, and this report should be read as a calibrated best judgment, not a benchmarked conclusion.
