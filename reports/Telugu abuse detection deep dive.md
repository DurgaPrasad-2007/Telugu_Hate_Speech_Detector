# Telugu detector: solid engineering, generic modeling

**Verdict: the detector is a well-executed, mainstream 2022-2025 baseline, not a modern or advanced system by October 2026 standards.** Its 0.9035 test macro-F1 (3 seeds) is in line with the 2022 MACD paper's own Telugu baselines, which run from about 0.86 (mBERT) and 0.865 (XLM-R) through 0.89 (MuRIL) and 0.894 (AbuseXLMR) to 0.900 (XLM-R joint training) ([Gupta et al., NeurIPS 2022](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/a7c4163b33286261b24c72fd3d1707c9-Paper-Datasets_and_Benchmarks.pdf)). The number is therefore neither suspiciously high nor a new state of the art. The 0.70-0.73 Telugu figures in other 2024-2025 papers come from harder, smaller Telugu-English code-mixed datasets and are not comparable. The modeling choices (XLM-R-base, LoRA r=8, LoRA matching full fine-tuning, TF-IDF+LinearSVC matching the transformer) are generic or are warning signs about the benchmark. The parts that are genuinely good are on the deployment and policy side: INT8 ONNX with a small loss, temperature calibration, an allow/review/block policy, and anti-obfuscation canonicalization. Even these are established patterns here, and none has been measured on Telugu stress data. No 2025-2026 paper evaluates a system like this on Telugu, so nothing contradicts the verdict and nothing certifies it. Three actions would move it most: a leakage and duplicate audit of the random 60:20:20 comment-level split, a baseline sweep (MuRIL, AbuseXLMR, IndicBERT, mmBERT) with paired significance tests, and a Telugu stress suite for the canonicalizer and the review band.

## The 0.9035 sits inside 2022 baselines, not above the field

The strongest published context is the MACD paper itself. Its random 60:20:20 monolingual Telugu results are mBERT 86.08, XLM-R 86.49, MuRIL 89.07 and AbuseXLMR 89.44 macro-F1, and XLM-R trained jointly on all five languages reaches 90.00 ([NeurIPS 2022 PDF](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/a7c4163b33286261b24c72fd3d1707c9-Paper-Datasets_and_Benchmarks.pdf)). The detector's 0.9035 is about 0.9 points above AbuseXLMR and 0.35 above the joint model. With a 3-seed SD of 0.0033 and no confirmation that the split and metric match, that margin does not support any ranking claim.

The MACD tables carry **conflicts that need checking in the PDF images**. Table 3 as extracted gives MuRIL 89.07 and XLM-R 86.49 for Telugu, while Table 7 labels 89.07 as "XLM-R (mono)", and the paper's prose says XLM-R is best for Hindi, Telugu and Malayalam among the three base models. Table 3 also holds two unlabelled Telugu values (89.42 and 91.37) whose rows could not be recovered. The label direction is also unclear: the README says 0 means abusive, which is the reverse of the usual convention. The Table 4 split mapping (80:10:10, chronological, unbalanced) is inferred, not read. Treat all MACD numbers above as provisional until checked against the table images.

The lower numbers in the literature do not contradict this. Kakarla and Venkata report 0.7314 macro-F1 for Hindi-Abusive-MuRIL on Telugu-English hate, but their PDF and HTML versions disagree, their captions are duplicated, and three models share identical scores of 0.3338, a signature of majority-class collapse ([arXiv 2502.10632](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.10632v1)). The 2024 Telugu code-mixed best system scored 0.7013 ([ACL 2024.dravidianlangtech-1.32](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2024.dravidianlangtech-1.32), snippet only). The closest like-for-like native-script Telugu result is X-MuTeST on HOLD-Telugu (4,492 comments): MuRIL with rationale supervision reached 0.8733 macro-F1, and zero-shot GPT-4o, Llama-3.1 and Mistral reached only 0.59-0.64 ([arXiv 2601.03194](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.03194)). That paper uses a different dataset and reports no seeds, so it brackets the detector without ranking it.

## What is generic: backbone, LoRA, TF-IDF parity, and the missing comparators

**XLM-R-base is the weakest defensible backbone for native-script Telugu.** On the IndicXTREME Telugu column, which averages across tasks and is not a classification score, XLM-R scores 56.4, MuRIL 64.2 and the "IndicBERT" row 70.2 ([arXiv 2212.05409](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.05409v3)). Mapping that row to IndicBERT v2 is an inference. MACD's own AbuseXLMR, which is XLM-R continued-pretrained on 5M social-media comments, beats base XLM-R on every dataset in the paper's cross-dataset table (for example Hindi HASOC 84.75 vs 82.39 macro-F1). It gains only about 0.4 points over MuRIL on MACD Telugu. The newer mmBERT includes Telugu (5.4B tokens, about 0.1-0.5% of each training stage) and beats XLM-R on XTREME averages (72.8 vs 70.4), but its paper has no Telugu-specific numbers ([arXiv 2509.06888](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.06888v1)). IndicBERT v3 exists on Hugging Face with no paper, language list or benchmark that I could find ([HF collection](https://huggingface.co/collections/ai4bharat/indicbert-v3)), so it is not yet a usable comparator. No source compares these encoders head-to-head on Telugu toxicity under one split. A reviewer will ask for MuRIL and AbuseXLMR at minimum, and the absence of a comparison is the detector's largest modeling gap.

**LoRA r=8 equal to full fine-tuning is routine, and the equality is within noise.** The closest controlled encoder study (XLM-R Large, multilingual news) found LoRA about 2 macro-F1 points behind full fine-tuning on one sub-task, with a LoRA SD of 6.3, and slightly ahead on another ([PLoS ONE 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11068208/)). Telugu hate work already used LoRA on encoders in early 2025 ([arXiv 2502.10632](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10632)). The detector's own full fine-tune scores 0.9057 ± 0.0033 against 0.9035 ± 0.0033 for LoRA, a 0.2-point gap far inside seed noise. A 278M-parameter encoder fits on one GPU, so LoRA is a convenience and a footprint choice, not an accuracy contribution. DoRA, AdaLoRA and IA3 have no verified encoder-classification evidence in the notes.

**TF-IDF+LinearSVC matching the transformer is the most informative result, and it points at the benchmark.** Across roughly a dozen comparisons in the notes, linear models are competitive in about a third, typically on small, lexically explicit, noisy data. No verified Telugu paper reports a paired TF-IDF-versus-transformer test. Two plausible readings fit the detector's result. Either the MACD Telugu labels are largely lexical, so any model that sees the abusive words reaches about 0.90, or comment-level random splits leak near-duplicates that n-grams exploit as easily as a transformer. The agreement score for Telugu labels is kappa 0.71, which also caps what any model can reach. Both readings imply the benchmark cannot separate models, and so cannot support claims about a better architecture, a better adapter or a better quantization.

**A leakage audit is the first priority.** MACD splits randomly 60:20:20 at the comment level, but its 152,422 comments sit under 92,881 posts, and comments on the same post share vocabulary and often text. The paper does not mention deduplication or a group split, and the notes found no published audit. Cross-dataset work shows the scale of the risk: a survey of 20 social-media NLP datasets documents duplication that overstates results ([arXiv 2410.03545](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03545), abstract only), and a Moj-challenge follow-up estimates 9-10% label error on different data ([arXiv 2605.22380](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.22380)); do not transfer that figure to MACD. Neither number applies to the detector until it runs exact and MinHash duplicate scans, a by-post group split, and a lexicon-only baseline.

## What is genuinely good: deployment, calibration, policy and canonicalization

**INT8 ONNX with a 1.2-point drop is normal and well executed.** The dynamic-quantization loss from 0.9042 to 0.8926 (about 1.3% relative) matches public XLM-R-base results: about 0.4 points F1 on MRPC ([Intel model card](https://huggingface.co/Intel/xlm-roberta-base-mrpc-int8-dynamic-inc)) and roughly 1.4 points on the XNLI average ([arXiv 2307.05972](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.05972), search summary). ONNX Runtime recommends dynamic quantization for transformers ([ORT docs](https://onnxruntime.ai/docs/performance/model-optimizations/quantization.html)), so this is the current standard, and shipping a CPU model with a measured loss and a deprecated broken MuRIL INT8 variant documented is better practice than most papers show. The caveat is statistical: one quantized run against a 3-seed FP32 mean cannot establish a 1-point effect. A paired bootstrap on the test set and a per-class breakdown are needed, and non-Latin-script languages are reported to suffer more from quantization in decoder LLMs ([Marchisio et al. 2024](https://preview.aclanthology.org/watermark/2024.findings-emnlp.935), indirect evidence).

**Calibration and a three-way policy match 2026 practice, but need workflow metrics.** Two-threshold auto-accept, human review and auto-reject routing is the established design ([arXiv 2601.05974](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05974v1)), and recent work frames moderation as a routing workflow whose key metric is the review share. A June 2026 paired study found 26.5% of decisions flip under Tamil-English code-mixing and review share rising from about 14% to about 30% when thresholds were tuned on clean English ([arXiv 2606.05654](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05654), preprint). Temperature scaling preserves argmax and so changes only where thresholds fall, and a May 2026 paper shows overall calibration can hide subgroup gaps ([arXiv 2605.14074](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.14074)). The detector should therefore report allow, review and block rates, precision inside each band, ECE with the binning stated, a risk-coverage curve, and subgroup checks on identity-term and code-mixed Telugu content. The specific thresholds are unvalidated: no authoritative threshold table exists for Telugu, and vendor rules of thumb such as 0.90 auto-accept are not evidence.

**Canonicalization is a sound idea, not a novelty, and it is untested on Telugu.** Normalization defenses date to the 2019 denoiser of Kurita et al., who showed character attacks cutting BERT toxicity recall from 0.914 to 0.597 on Jigsaw ([arXiv 1912.06872](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.06872)), and to the rule-based Adversarial Text Normalizer of 2022 ([arXiv 2206.04137](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.04137), abstract only; no before/after figures read). Plain NFC/NFKC does not stop cross-script homoglyphs, which need confusable mapping, per a secondary summary only ([translit docs](https://translit.readthedocs.io/en/latest/security/adversarial-defense.html)). Telugu-specific risks run both ways: the notes found no paper applying rule-based canonicalization to Telugu, and aggressive folding could damage Telugu combining marks, an untested inference. Romanized Telugu spelling variation, which no fixed character rule covers, is the larger open vector, and a 2026 benchmark argues single-canonical-transliteration evaluations overstate robustness ([MRL 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.mrl-1.29/)). The defense is real engineering; its value stays unproven until a stress suite shows clean-text false-positive cost and attack recovery.

## Where the field is moving, and what to do about it

The 2025-2026 field has not abandoned fine-tuned encoders for this task. Zero-shot GPT-4o, Llama-3.1 and Mistral trail fine-tuned MuRIL by about 0.2-0.3 macro-F1 on Telugu and Hindi in the one table-level comparison ([arXiv 2601.03194](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.03194)), and a WOAH 2025 study reports prompting lagging fine-tuned encoders on most real-world sets ([ACL 2025.woah-1.39](https://aclanthology.org/2025.woah-1.39/), abstract only). No fine-tuned-LLM-on-Telugu result exists in the notes. Guard models do not fill the gap: PolyGuard's only Indic language is Hindi ([arXiv 2504.04377](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.04377v1)), and Qwen3Guard, Llama Guard 4 and IndicGuard have no Telugu numbers I could read. The newest techniques that would read as modern are rationale-supervised encoders, domain-adapted checkpoints such as AbuseXLMR or Hindi-Abusive-MuRIL, small ensembles of 2-4 Indic encoders (the dominant shared-task recipe), and robustness evaluation.

Ranked by value for effort, the concrete steps are as follows.

1. **Audit the benchmark.** Run exact and MinHash/Jaccard (about 0.7-0.8, state the value) duplicate scans between train and test after Telugu script normalization. Re-split by post ID using MACD's metadata, compare random against grouped scores, add a lexicon-only baseline, inspect the top SVM n-grams, confirm the label direction on the CSV, and re-score every model on the cleaned test set. This explains the TF-IDF parity and tells you whether 0.90 is a ceiling or an artifact.
2. **Add the missing comparators on the same splits.** Run MuRIL, AbuseXLMR (verify its checkpoint and licence), IndicBERT v2 and mmBERT with 5 seeds each. Report mean ± SD, a paired bootstrap of macro-F1 differences with a 95% interval, McNemar on paired predictions, and the TF-IDF char n-gram baseline. Call differences smaller than the seed SD ties, including LoRA versus full fine-tuning and FP32 versus INT8.
3. **Build a small Telugu stress and fairness suite.** Include homoglyph, zero-width, leetspeak, Romanized Telugu spelling variants and typo perturbations, plus HateCheck-style Telugu contrast cases (negation, quoted or reclaimed slurs, identity terms in benign use). Measure model alone versus model plus canonicalizer, and canonicalizer false positives on clean text. Estimate label noise by re-annotating about 200 items with a second annotator.
4. **Evaluate the policy as a workflow.** Choose thresholds on a held-out in-language set, then report allow/review/block rates, band precision, automatic error rate and a risk-coverage curve, separately for pure Telugu and code-mixed inputs. Check calibration per subgroup, since temperature scaling struggles when miscalibration is uneven.
5. **Add a few-shot LLM reference and an ensemble.** A modest Gemma or Qwen few-shot arm and a two-to-three encoder soft-vote ensemble cover what reviewers now expect, though neither is likely to beat the tuned encoder at CPU cost.
6. **Test portability.** The README states code-mixed data is not evaluated. Add the 2,000-comment Telugu-English set only as a diagnostic, after resolving its table-versus-text conflict (Table 5 shows the LLM at 0.907 and classical models near 0.5, while the text reports 0.77-0.84) ([arXiv 2504.21026](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.21026v1)).

## Unverified items and conflicts to carry forward

Most PDFs in this research did not decode, so many figures come from HTML extraction or search snippets. Before citing any of the following, check the primary source. The MACD Table 3 versus Table 7 label conflict, the unlabelled 89.42/91.37 Telugu values and the Table 4 and 6 row assignments are unresolved. The Kakarla and Venkata PDF and HTML tables disagree, and their captions are duplicated. The Pandey et al. table and text contradict each other. For the 2026 DravidianLangTech Telugu communicative-style task, one summary says a MuRIL system placed 7th (macro-F1 0.1703) while another says IndicBERT v2 with partial freezing took the top rank, and neither could be reconciled. The Telugu-BERT 0.77 F1 attribution, mmBERT low-resource gains quoted by third parties, IndicGuard's Telugu coverage, and the Q8BERT per-task quantization drops (secondary numbers conflict) are also unverified. The IndicXTREME "IndicBERT" row is probably v2 but is not confirmed, and the 70.2 / 64.2 / 56.4 figures are task averages, not toxicity scores. Latency on a low-end CPU was never measured; the README reports it only on the development PC. A claim that the pipeline is "advanced" would rest entirely on items 1-4 above, which are cheap to run and not yet done.

## Conclusion

The detector is best described as a competent reproduction of the 2022 MACD recipe with unusually careful engineering around it. Its accuracy cannot be separated from a dataset where a linear model ties a transformer and a 60:20:20 comment-level split may leak, so the strongest honest claim today is "reproduces published MACD Telugu baselines with a CPU-deployable, calibrated moderation stack," not "state of the art." The novel angle available to this project is not a better backbone: it is being the first to show, on Telugu, whether canonicalization, calibrated abstention and the review band survive obfuscation, Romanization and code-mixing. The literature has no Telugu answer to those questions, and the 2026 workflow-instability and Romanization-variation papers show that the field is now asking them.

A clean leakage audit could also change the story in either direction. If deduplicated, group-split scores fall well below 0.90, the TF-IDF parity, the LoRA parity and the INT8 comparison all need restating. If they hold, the project would have the more interesting finding: that a lexical baseline suffices for MACD Telugu and that the gains must come from robustness and policy, not model scale.
